# Review of Related Literature

## 1. Background of the Study

Citrus production is a locally relevant agricultural concern for Nueva Vizcaya because the province is recognized as a major citrus-producing area in the Philippines. Idquival, Layog, Victoria, and Rosete (2023) identified Nueva Vizcaya as the country's main citrus producer and discussed how the citrus industry has been affected by land availability, temperature, precipitation, production cost, pests, disease, and climate-related pressures. Their analysis also noted that Kasibu, Nueva Vizcaya has land devoted to citrus cultivation and produces varieties that include Hamlin, Perante, and Valencia oranges. The Philippine Information Agency (2023) also reported that Vizcaya orange varieties are produced and sold from August to November, including Valencia and seedless Hamlin orange, also known locally as Perante Orange. DOST-PCAARRD (2018) further reported that citrus farming in Malabing Valley, Kasibu has contributed to employment and farmer livelihood in Nueva Vizcaya.

Because of this local importance, farmers need reliable ways to monitor the condition of their citrus trees. In a Perante orange orchard, the farmer may need to know how many fruits are visible on each registered tree and which areas of the orchard have higher or lower fruit load. This information can help with yield estimation, labor planning, selling decisions, harvest scheduling, and farm record keeping. However, checking every tree manually can take a long time, especially when a farm has many trees. Manual checking may also vary from one person to another because fruit counting depends on the observer's position, eyesight, patience, and access to different sides of the tree.

One major difficulty is that fruits are not arranged on a simple flat surface. Perante oranges grow around a three-dimensional canopy, meaning some fruits can be seen from the front, some from the left or right side, and some from the back. Some fruits may also be hidden behind leaves, branches, or other fruits. A person standing in one position may count only the fruits visible from that side, while missing fruits located deeper inside the canopy or on the opposite side. In the same way, a camera or drone that captures only one photo of a tree may not see the full fruit distribution. Therefore, one image is usually not enough to represent the whole tree.

A drone can help by taking images from different angles while moving around the tree. Instead of relying on one viewpoint, the drone may circle the tree and capture several photos or record a video. This can give the system more visual information because fruits hidden from one angle may become visible from another angle. For example, an orange hidden behind leaves in the front view may become visible when the drone moves to the side. This is why circular scanning is useful for the proposed project: it gives the computer vision model a better chance to observe visible fruits around the canopy.

However, using many photos or a video also creates another problem. If the drone detects the same orange in one frame and then detects that same orange again in the next frame, the system may count it twice. For example, if one Perante orange appears in five consecutive video frames, a simple fruit detector may produce five detections even though there is only one real fruit. This problem is called repeated counting or duplicate counting across frames. It is important because a fruit detector by itself only identifies fruit-like objects inside an image; it does not automatically know whether a fruit in the current image is the same physical fruit seen in a previous image.

This means that the project cannot depend only on simple frame-by-frame detection. If every detected orange in every frame is added directly to the total count, the result may become too high. The system needs a way to connect detections across images. In computer vision, this can be done through object tracking, where the system follows the same detected fruit across nearby frames, or through multi-view association, where the system estimates whether detections from different angles belong to the same real fruit. Wang, Underwood, and Walsh (2019), for example, used MangoYOLO with a Kalman filter and the Hungarian algorithm to associate mango detections between neighboring video frames and reduce repeated counting. Liu et al. (2018) also combined deep learning, frame-to-frame tracking, and structure from motion to count fruits more reliably across image sequences.

Another difficulty is that the drone is moving while the tree, leaves, and fruits may also appear to move because of wind. Outdoor lighting can change as clouds pass or as sunlight creates shadows on the canopy. A fruit may be clear in one frame, blurry in another frame, and partly covered in the next frame. These field conditions make fruit detection more difficult than detecting objects in a controlled indoor image. Maldonado and Barbosa (2016) studied automatic green fruit counting in orange trees using digital images and showed that image-based counting can estimate visible fruit numbers, but the task remains affected by visibility and image conditions. These limitations show why the proposed system should include confidence values and should not claim perfect accuracy under all orchard conditions.

The need for localized dataset collection is also supported by the literature on agricultural computer vision. Bargoti and Underwood (2017) showed that data augmentation can reduce the number of required training images, but their orchard results still depended on domain-relevant examples and appropriate annotation practices. Hani, Roy, and Isler (2020) introduced MinneApple to make apple detection and segmentation more comparable across methods, demonstrating the importance of a clearly defined benchmark dataset. For Perante orange detection, public fruit datasets may provide useful starting points, but they cannot fully represent Kasibu-specific conditions such as Perante fruit color, fruit size, canopy density, sunlight, background vegetation, camera distance, and local orchard layout. The proposed project therefore requires local image and video collection from Perante orange trees, followed by a custom annotation workflow.

In this project, Databox is positioned as the dataset and annotation framework for organizing the local training corpus. Databox supports the research requirement to store image metadata, tree identifiers, review status, and bounding-box labels for `citrus_fruit` and `citrus_canopy`, then export the dataset in YOLO format. The canopy annotation is not merely a convenience label. It gives the model and downstream pipeline a tree-centered region of interest, helping the system distinguish the target canopy from nearby trees, background leaves, sky, trunk, or ground. This is important because citrus orchards may contain overlapping tree canopies, and a drone orbit scan can easily include fruit from adjacent trees unless the target canopy is explicitly represented. In this sense, Databox supports both machine-learning reproducibility and local agronomic relevance: the image set, label vocabulary, canopy bounding boxes, and exported annotations are aligned with the actual Perante orange inspection task.

For these reasons, the present project should be framed as an autonomous drone-based system for estimating visible Perante orange count and supporting tree-level orchard mapping. The word "visible" is important because some fruits may be fully hidden inside the canopy and cannot be detected by an RGB camera. The system can still be useful if it provides consistent tree-level estimates, stores inspection records, identifies trees with higher fruit load, and helps farmers review orchard conditions more efficiently. The research problem therefore involves not only detecting fruits, but also deciding how the drone should capture the tree, how repeated detections should be handled, how tree registration should guide inspection, and how the final count should be reported in a way that is useful and honest for farm decision-making.

## 2. Computer Vision, YOLO Detection, 360-Degree Tracking, and Yield Estimation

Earlier computer-vision approaches for fruit detection used hand-crafted color, texture, and shape features, while more recent studies rely heavily on deep learning. Sa, Ge, Dayoub, Upcroft, Perez, and McCool (2016) developed DeepFruits, a fruit detection system using deep convolutional neural networks and Faster R-CNN for sweet pepper, rock melon, almond, apple, avocado, mango, and orange-like orchard targets. Their study showed that deep neural networks can provide accurate fruit detection in agricultural environments, making them useful for robotic harvesting, yield estimation, and autonomous agricultural platforms.

Bargoti and Underwood (2017) applied Faster R-CNN to fruit detection in orchards containing mangoes, almonds, and apples. They reported that data augmentation reduced the number of required training images by more than half and that tiling helped process orchard images containing hundreds of fruits. This is relevant to Perante orange detection because citrus-canopy images may contain many small fruit targets at different scales. Their results imply that the present project should include augmentation, multi-scale image handling, and local fine-tuning instead of relying only on a generic pretrained model.

Koirala, Walsh, Wang, and McCarthy (2019) benchmarked six deep learning architectures for mango detection and introduced MangoYOLO for real-time fruit detection and orchard fruit-load estimation. Their work is important because it demonstrated the suitability of YOLO-style detectors for real-time agricultural monitoring. Wang et al. (2019) extended this direction by combining MangoYOLO with Kalman filtering and the Hungarian algorithm to track mangoes across video frames, reducing repeated counting of the same fruit. For the present project, these findings suggest that a drone video workflow should not simply add frame-by-frame detections; it should use tracking or multi-view association to reduce double counting while the drone circles the tree.

Modern YOLO models are especially relevant for the proposed system because they are designed for real-time object detection and can be fine-tuned on custom datasets. YOLOv8 was released by Ultralytics in 2023 as a practical family of object detection, segmentation, classification, and pose models (Jocher, Chaurasia, & Qiu, 2023; Ultralytics, n.d.-a). YOLOv11 later introduced architectural refinements such as C3k2 modules and attention-based improvements for feature extraction and efficiency (Khanam & Hussain, 2024; Ultralytics, n.d.-b). Recent agricultural work has begun comparing YOLOv8 through YOLOv11 in fruit detection settings. Karacaoglu and Sahin (2025), for example, benchmarked YOLOv8 to YOLOv11 architectures for kiwi detection in densely occluded orchard conditions and found that lightweight optimized YOLO models can provide a useful accuracy-efficiency trade-off for embedded agricultural systems. In citrus-specific work, Jrondi, Moussaid, and Hadi (2024) compared transformer-based detection with YOLOv8 for citrus fruit detection within trees, while Lin et al. (2024) and Cong et al. (2025) show continued YOLO-based citrus research under complex orchard and fruit-surface conditions.

These studies justify using YOLOv8 as a realistic baseline model for the Perante orange prototype, with YOLOv11 as an updated candidate model for comparison if training time and hardware allow. YOLOv8 is attractive because it is widely adopted, easy to train with YOLO-formatted labels from Databox, and supported by common evaluation metrics such as precision, recall, F1-score, and mean average precision. YOLOv11 is relevant because its newer architecture may improve feature extraction and small-object recognition, which matter when citrus fruits appear small, partly occluded, or visually similar to sunlit leaves. The appropriate model choice should therefore be empirical: the system should train and evaluate the candidate model on locally collected Perante orange data rather than assuming that a model trained on apples, mangoes, or generic citrus images will transfer perfectly to Kasibu orchards.

Hani, Roy, and Isler (2020) compared different fruit detection and counting methods for apple yield mapping and explained that detecting fruits correctly in an image does not automatically mean that the final fruit count will be correct. If a camera or drone captures several images while moving along a tree row, the same fruit may appear in more than one image. If the system simply adds all detections from all images, the same fruit may be counted repeatedly. Because of this, their study emphasized the need for end-to-end counting methods, meaning the system should not only detect fruits but also consider camera movement, repeated observations, and the relationship between detections from different views. Hani, Roy, and Isler (2020) also introduced MinneApple as a benchmark dataset where fruits are annotated so computer vision models can be trained and compared more fairly. For a Perante orange project, this reinforces the need to collect and label local Perante orange images because Perante orange color, fruit size, canopy density, camera angle, lighting, and orchard background in Nueva Vizcaya may differ from those datasets.

Liu et al. (2019) proposed a monocular-camera fruit counting and mapping pipeline that used deep neural networks, semantic structure from motion, and semantic data association. Their study is relevant because it showed that a low-cost camera-based system can support fruit counting and mapping without always requiring expensive LiDAR and GPS/INS sensor suites. Liu et al. (2018) also combined deep segmentation, frame-to-frame tracking, and structure from motion to count fruit from image sequences, using Kalman-filter-assisted tracking and the Hungarian algorithm to limit duplicate counts. These studies support the idea that the project should connect fruit detection with camera pose, mapping, and tracking instead of treating each image as an isolated observation.

Recent citrus-specific studies further confirm that orange and citrus counting has already been researched. Zhang et al. (2022) proposed deep-learning-based in-field citrus fruit detection and tracking, using OrangeYOLO and OrangeSort to detect and track citrus fruits in orchard conditions. Zheng et al. (2023) proposed an online citrus counting system for large-scale unstructured orchards using UAVs and designed Citrus-YOLO for real-time citrus detection in video streams. These studies show that research on citrus fruit detection already exists internationally, including UAV-based citrus counting, but they also leave room for a localized Perante orange system that integrates farmer tree registration, autonomous circular scanning, duplicate-count reduction, orchard mapping, and a dashboard for tree-level records.

### 2.1 Duplicate Counting During 360-Degree Circular Orbit Scans

Standard multi-object tracking methods are useful but incomplete for a drone that performs a 360-degree circular orbit around one tree. SORT uses Kalman filtering and frame-to-frame association through bounding-box overlap (Bewley et al., 2016), while DeepSORT adds an appearance descriptor to improve identity preservation during occlusion or temporary disappearance (Wojke, Bewley, & Paulus, 2017). ByteTrack improves association by using both high-score and low-score detections, allowing some weak detections to remain useful for tracking (Zhang et al., 2022). These algorithms are valuable for many video-tracking tasks and can help reduce duplicate counting between neighboring frames.

However, a Perante orange circular scan presents a harder geometric problem than ordinary 2D tracking. During an orbit, the drone changes its viewing angle continuously around the canopy. The same fruit may move from one side of the image to the other, become temporarily hidden by leaves, reappear from a different angle, or change its apparent size and shape because of perspective. The background behind the fruit also changes drastically as the drone moves. A fruit may be lit by direct sunlight from one angle and shadowed from another. These effects can break 2D intersection-over-union association because the fruit's bounding box may not overlap with its previous bounding box after a large viewpoint change. They can also weaken appearance-based re-identification because orange fruits are visually similar to one another, and their color/texture may change with exposure, blur, and partial occlusion.

For this reason, a pure 2D tracker should be treated as a local short-term association tool, not as the sole solution for 360-degree duplicate reduction. ByteTrack, DeepSORT, Kalman filtering, and Hungarian matching can associate detections across adjacent frames when viewpoint changes are small, but they are likely to fail across wider orbit angles. The limitation is not simply that the algorithms are weak; rather, their assumptions are insufficient for circular orchard inspection. A detector output is a 2D box on a moving image plane, while the real fruit is a 3D object fixed on the tree canopy. When the drone orbits, parallax makes the 2D motion of nearby leaves, branches, and fruits differ from background motion. Thus, duplicate-count reduction must be linked to camera pose and spatial projection.

The literature on camera geometry and fruit mapping supports this need for multi-view spatial reasoning. Hartley and Zisserman (2004) established the geometric foundation for relating 2D image points to 3D camera rays through camera calibration, projection matrices, and epipolar geometry. Liu et al. (2019) applied this principle in a fruit-counting context by estimating fruit landmarks in 3D and using semantic data association to reduce double counting. Dong, Roy, and Isler (2018) also showed that orchard mapping can benefit from semantic 3D reconstruction, using visual data to estimate canopy volume, tree height, and fruit count in orchard environments. These works suggest that the proposed Perante orange system should combine image detections with drone pose instead of relying only on image-space overlap.

In the proposed architecture, each YOLO detection should be associated with available drone telemetry and camera metadata. This includes camera intrinsics, image size, estimated camera orientation, gimbal or camera mounting angle, drone altitude, GPS or local position, heading, and mission time. In an ArduPilot-based system, this information can be accessed or synchronized through MAVLink messages, while the Raspberry Pi camera or ground processing engine provides image timestamps and detection outputs. A pose-guided ray-casting approach can then project the center of each 2D fruit bounding box into a 3D ray from the camera toward the tree canopy. If the target tree center and approximate canopy radius are known from farmer registration, GPS, local map coordinates, or canopy detection, the system can intersect or approximate the ray against a canopy surface or local tree-centered coordinate frame. Detections that project to nearby 3D locations around the same tree can be treated as likely observations of the same fruit.

This method does not require the thesis to claim exact 3D fruit mapping of every orange. Rather, it provides a principled duplicate-reduction layer. The system may use 2D tracking for adjacent frames, then use pose-guided clustering for detections that reappear after larger orbit angles. Each detection can be represented as a tuple containing tree ID, timestamp, image ID, 2D bounding box, model confidence, camera pose, projected ray, approximate canopy intersection, and cluster ID. The final visible count can then be calculated from unique fruit clusters rather than raw detection totals. This approach directly addresses the central weakness of circular scanning: the same fruit can be seen many times from different angles, but it belongs to one physical location on or near the canopy.

### 2.2 Visible Fruit Count, Total Yield, and Canopy Occlusion

The proposed project should distinguish between visible fruit count and total tree yield. Let $N_v$ represent the estimated number of unique visible fruits detected during the drone scan after duplicate reduction. Let $\hat{Y}$ represent an estimated total yield or total fruit count for the tree. These are not the same. $N_v$ is bounded by camera visibility, lighting, occlusion, detection confidence, and the quality of the circular scan. $\hat{Y}$ attempts to infer hidden fruit as well, which requires additional agronomic or empirical modeling.

Orchard yield-estimation literature supports this distinction. Wang, Nuske, Bergerman, and Singh (2013) developed an automated apple yield-estimation system using stereo imaging and sequential image registration, showing that automated fruit counts can support crop-load estimation. Cheng, Damerow, Sun, and Blanke (2017) used image analysis of apple fruit and tree-canopy features with neural networks for early yield prediction, explicitly linking visible image-derived fruit measures with canopy-related features. He et al. (2022) reviewed direct and indirect methods for orchard fruit yield prediction and estimation, showing that yield models may use fruit counts, canopy features, spectral measurements, and other variables depending on the crop and sensing system.

For Perante oranges, the most defensible first output is therefore $N_v$, the visible fruit count per registered tree. A later yield model can estimate $\hat{Y}$ using empirical regression calibrated against field-counted samples. A simple conceptual model can be written as:

$$
\hat{Y} = \beta_0 + \beta_1N_v + \beta_2V + \beta_3\rho + \epsilon
$$

where $V$ is estimated canopy volume, $\rho$ is a foliage-density or occlusion proxy, and $\epsilon$ represents unexplained variation. Canopy volume may be approximated from tree height and canopy radius, from image-based canopy bounding boxes, or from 3D reconstruction if sufficient multi-view data are available. Foliage density may be estimated from canopy segmentation, visible leaf area, image texture, or the ratio between canopy area and visible fruit detections. The purpose of this model is not to replace field validation; rather, it provides a formal way to acknowledge that total yield estimation must account for hidden fruit and canopy structure. In the thesis prototype, $N_v$ should remain the primary reported metric, while $\hat{Y}$ should be described as an empirical extension requiring calibration from manually counted validation trees.

## 3. Autonomous UAV Navigation, ArduPilot, and Range-Based Orbiting in Orchards

UAVs are useful for orchard monitoring because they can capture images from viewpoints that may be difficult for a ground worker or ground robot to reach. However, orchard flight is difficult because trees, branches, uneven terrain, wind, GPS error, and narrow spaces increase collision risk. Stefas, Bayram, and Isler (2019) studied vision-based monitoring of orchards with UAVs and developed a system for navigating inside orchard rows for crop inspection and yield-estimation tasks. Their work is directly related to the present project because it connects UAV navigation with orchard monitoring rather than using drones only for high-altitude mapping.

Hulens, Ribbens, Goedeme, and De Cubber (2015) studied real-time vision-based UAV navigation in fruit orchards using low-cost sensors such as a webcam. Their approach detected the center and end of orchard corridors so the UAV could move through rows without colliding with trees. This supports the idea that the project can begin with structured orchard navigation, waypoint guidance, and controlled scan behavior before attempting full autonomy in an unknown farm.

Yang, Ibrahimov, and Mueller (2025) presented an autonomous aerial system for fruit counting inside orchard canopies. Their study is useful because it did not treat fruit counting as only a computer vision task where an image is given to an AI model. Instead, they treated it as a complete drone workflow. The drone first needs a route to follow, which is handled by global path planning. It also needs to estimate its own movement and position while flying, which they handled through visual-inertial odometry. While moving near branches and leaves, the drone must avoid obstacles, so their system used depth-image-based local planning. After useful images were captured, YOLOv8 was used to detect fruits, ByteTrack was used to track fruits across video frames so the same fruit would not be counted repeatedly, and COLMAP was used for 3D reconstruction from multiple images. This is relevant to the present project because it shows that orchard fruit counting is not solved by fruit detection alone. A drone-based Perante orange system also needs image capture, navigation, obstacle avoidance, fruit detection, tracking, and some form of mapping or 3D understanding so that the final visible fruit count is more reliable.

GPS-based waypoint navigation is useful for approaching a registered tree from a launch area, but it is not sufficient for close inspection near dense foliage. Orchard canopies can weaken satellite visibility, and branches or leaves can obstruct line-of-sight navigation and produce safety risks. Cui, Lai, Dong, and Chen (2016) studied autonomous UAV navigation in foliage environments without GPS using a 2D laser range finder, Kalman-filter-based state estimation, pose-graph optimization, and online path planning. Their work demonstrates that local sensing is essential when a UAV operates close to vegetation. In the proposed project, this supports a dual-tier navigation approach: global GPS or map waypoints can bring the drone near the selected Perante orange tree, while local sensing must control the close-range orbit.

For the specific behavior of circling a tree, UAV circumnavigation research provides useful control ideas. Cao, Muse, Casbeer, and Kingston (2013) proposed control algorithms that allow a UAV to circumnavigate an unknown target using range and range-rate measurements while maintaining a desired orbit radius. Cao (2015) further studied UAV circumnavigation in GPS-denied conditions using range-only measurement. These studies are not citrus-specific, but they provide a control-theory basis for the present project's tree-orbit behavior: the drone can maintain a desired distance from a target tree, move tangentially around it, and keep the tree inside the camera view.

Chen et al. (2023) developed a quadcopter drone for vision-based autonomous target following and obstacle avoidance. Their work supports the use of onboard camera information for target tracking, while obstacle avoidance remains active during flight. For a Perante orange inspection system, this means the circular scanning behavior should combine target-centered orbiting, distance keeping, image capture at multiple angles, and safety constraints around branches or nearby trees.

The proposed hardware and software architecture should therefore use two levels of control. First, a global mission layer uses farmer-registered tree coordinates and orchard-map records to plan an approach route. This can use GPS, compass heading, altitude, and mission waypoints. Second, a local orbit layer uses range sensors such as a LiDAR/ToF rangefinder, TFmini Plus-type distance sensor, optical flow, or camera-based canopy localization to maintain a safe distance from the target canopy. The local layer is necessary because the orbit is not only a navigation maneuver; it is also a data-capture trajectory. The drone should maintain enough distance to avoid branches, keep the canopy in view, and collect image frames from distributed angles around the tree.

The physical sensor choice must also account for outdoor optical limitations. Low-cost 940 nm time-of-flight sensors such as the VL53L0X and optical-flow/lidar modules that integrate similar short-range infrared ranging, such as the Matek 3901-L0X, are attractive for indoor hovering because they are small, lightweight, and inexpensive. However, outdoor citrus inspection exposes these sensors to strong solar infrared irradiance and highly variable leaf reflectance. Kazmi, Foix, Alenya, and Andersen (2014) compared time-of-flight and stereo vision sensors for indoor and outdoor leaf imaging and showed that plant depth imaging is strongly affected by illumination conditions, with active ToF sensing requiring careful treatment under outdoor lighting. ArduPilot documentation likewise warns that VL53L0X/VL53L1X range and precision are significantly reduced in bright sunlight and that these sensors are not recommended for outdoor use (ArduPilot Development Team, n.d.-d). This supports the project's transition from an indoor/low-light optical-flow and ToF assumption toward a hybrid outdoor navigation scheme where a GPS and compass module, such as the HGLRC M100-5883, provides global position, heading, altitude-hold support, return-to-home capability, and approach navigation, while local range sensing is treated as supplemental and condition-dependent near the canopy.

ArduPilot, particularly ArduCopter, is appropriate for this research architecture because it is a mature open-source autopilot ecosystem with MAVLink support, mission planning, telemetry, companion-computer integration, simulation, and a large research/user community. Aliane (2024) surveyed open-source UAV autopilots and compared hardware compatibility, software features, communication protocols, companion-computer integration, ROS compatibility, and SITL/HITL simulation support. This type of comparison supports the selection of ArduPilot or PX4 for autonomous research systems, while also clarifying why FPV-oriented firmware is less suitable for the full thesis pipeline. Betaflight is widely used for manual and racing-oriented multirotor flight, but its design priority is responsive pilot control rather than waypoint missions, MAVLink-based robotic integration, and SITL-based autonomy testing. iNav offers GPS and navigation features for hobby and long-range use, but it is less aligned with the proposed research need for ROS 2/Gazebo simulation, structured MAVLink telemetry, and companion-computer mission integration. PX4 is also a strong research autopilot, but ArduPilot is especially attractive here because ArduCopter has broad vehicle support, MAVLink tooling, rangefinder integration, mission commands, Lua scripting, and official Gazebo/SITL paths that match the project's simulation-first development plan.

The companion-computer split architecture further supports the physical hardware design. In a micro-UAV, low-level stabilization and motor control require deterministic, low-latency behavior; therefore, these tasks are normally assigned to a dedicated flight controller, while higher-level perception, sensor interfacing, telemetry routing, and mission logic are assigned to a companion computer. PX4 documentation describes this general architecture as a flight controller connected to a companion computer for computationally expensive functions such as collision prevention (PX4 Development Team, n.d.-a), and ArduPilot documentation similarly explains that companion computers communicate with the flight controller through MAVLink, receive autopilot telemetry including GPS data, and can trigger sensing or preprocessing tasks during flight (ArduPilot Development Team, n.d.-c). Koubaa et al. (2019) describe MAVLink as a lightweight bidirectional protocol used by ArduPilot and PX4 for monitoring, control, and integration of unmanned systems. These sources justify separating the SpeedyBee F405 Mini flight-control role from the Raspberry Pi Zero 2 W companion-computer role: the flight controller running ArduPilot or iNav handles stabilization and safety-critical flight loops, while the Raspberry Pi captures images, relays telemetry, performs protocol translation, and coordinates data upload.

This split is especially important because the proposed drone is a small, weight-limited platform. Running full YOLO inference, 3D reconstruction, and dashboard synchronization entirely onboard would increase sustained CPU load, power draw, heat, and memory pressure. Koubaa, Ammar, Alahdab, Kanhouch, and Azar (2020) experimentally evaluated cloud computation offloading and edge computing for deep learning in Internet-connected drones and noted that UAVs have limited energy capabilities while computer vision and deep learning require substantial computational resources. Benoit-Cattin, Velasco-Montero, and Fernandez-Berni (2020) further showed that sustained CNN inference on a Raspberry Pi-class edge device can cause thermal throttling and frame-rate degradation without active cooling. These findings support the project's architecture in which the Raspberry Pi Zero 2 W performs lightweight capture and communication tasks, while heavy neural network inference and multi-view post-processing are offloaded to a ground laptop or cloud engine. This does not remove the value of onboard computing; rather, it assigns onboard computing to the tasks that fit the thermal, memory, and power envelope of a micro-drone.

The frame design is also supported by collision-tolerant micro aerial vehicle literature. Close-range canopy inspection increases the likelihood of accidental contact with leaves, small branches, or surrounding orchard structures. De Petris, Carlson, Papachristos, and Alexis (2022) reviewed collision-tolerant aerial robots and argued that collision tolerance becomes important as aerial robots operate in cluttered environments where impacts may not always be avoidable. De Azambuja, Fouad, Bouteiller, Sol, and Beltrame (2022) introduced CogniFly as a sub-250 g open-source collision-resilient quadcopter inspired by arthropod exoskeletons, using a semi-rigid structure with soft joints to absorb and distribute impact energy. Abazari, Bakir, Sertpoyraz, and Ozcan (2025) experimentally analyzed structural compliance in multirotor micro aerial vehicles and found that softer inner-frame structures can increase impact duration and reduce impact acceleration during collisions. These findings justify a lightweight 3D-printed, compliant frame paradigm for the Perante orange inspection prototype. Compared with a purely rigid carbon-fiber racing-style frame, a TPU-based protective structure can dampen contact forces and reduce the risk of damaging the drone or tree during cautious canopy-centered scanning, while preserving a low-cost and locally manufacturable design path.

This does not mean that early prototype flights must immediately implement full autonomy. A staged approach is more realistic: initial physical testing may validate manual flight, camera capture, and safety behavior; later integration can move toward ArduPilot/MAVLink-based autonomous capture and circular scans. The literature supports this phased strategy because orchard autonomy combines several difficult subproblems: flight stabilization, localization, obstacle avoidance, target-centered orbiting, image capture, fruit detection, duplicate reduction, and map updating. Treating ArduPilot as the research autopilot layer gives the project a path from simulation to field deployment without isolating flight control from the computer vision and dashboard pipeline.

## 4. Simulation Environment for Orchard Flight Testing

Simulation is important for this project because real drone testing near trees can be risky, weather-dependent, and expensive. Gazebo is a widely used open-source robotics simulator that supports physics engines, realistic 3D rendering, sensor simulation, plugins, robot models, and command-line tools (Open Robotics, n.d.). Gazebo Classic documentation also states that it can generate sensor data from cameras, depth cameras, laser range finders, contact sensors, and other simulated sensors, which is useful for testing navigation, obstacle avoidance, and computer vision pipelines before field deployment.

PX4 documentation describes Gazebo as an open-source robotics simulator and identifies support for quadrotor, plane, VTOL, and rover simulation in software-in-the-loop workflows (PX4 Development Team, n.d.-b). Furrer, Burri, Achtelik, and Siegwart (2016) introduced RotorS as a modular Gazebo-based micro aerial vehicle simulator that supports multirotor models, controllers, state estimation, and simulated sensors. Xiao et al. (2020) also presented XTDrone, a customizable multi-rotor UAV simulation platform based on ROS, Gazebo, and PX4 for testing SLAM, object detection, motion planning, control, and multi-UAV cooperation. These studies support the choice of Gazebo or Gazebo with autopilot/ROS integration as a simulation environment for the proposed citrus orchard drone system.

For the proposed ArduPilot-centered workflow, Gazebo Sim with ArduPilot SITL provides a more direct path from simulated autonomy to ArduCopter mission testing. ArduPilot documentation describes SITL with Gazebo as a way to use Gazebo as an external simulator for ArduPilot Rover, Copter, and Plane (ArduPilot Development Team, n.d.-a). The `ardupilot_gz` project provides ROS 2 packages for simulating models controlled by ArduPilot SITL with DDS support in Gazebo, including packages for robot description, Gazebo plugins, ROS 2 application code, and launch utilities (ArduPilot, n.d.; ArduPilot Development Team, n.d.-b). This is directly relevant because the project needs to test not only a flying vehicle model but also autonomous mission behavior, telemetry streams, camera viewpoints, range sensors, and orchard obstacles.

For the present project, Gazebo can be used to construct a simulated citrus orchard with virtual Perante orange-like trees, terrain, obstacles, drone waypoints, camera sensors, and circular tree-scanning paths. The simulation can help test whether the drone can approach a registered tree, maintain a safe circular path, avoid obstacles, capture images from multiple angles, and return to a launch area. It can also support repeated experiments that would be difficult to perform safely in a real farm during early development. ROS 2 can provide the middleware for connecting simulated camera data, tree targets, map updates, and mission-state logic, while ArduPilot SITL can provide the flight-control and MAVLink telemetry layer. Together, Gazebo Sim, `ardupilot_gz`, ROS 2, and ArduPilot SITL create a software-in-the-loop architecture that allows the team to validate terrain behavior, canopy obstacles, circular orbit trajectories, sensor assumptions, and dashboard mission updates before field deployment.

The simulation environment should therefore be treated as part of the research method, not merely as a demonstration. It can generate repeatable test cases such as a tree with clear canopy access, a tree partly blocked by another tree, a sloped terrain approach, a near-branch obstacle, or a failed GPS condition. These scenarios can be used to evaluate navigation success rate, orbit completion, minimum obstacle distance, image coverage around the tree, and whether the system updates scan missions correctly. Simulation cannot replace real Perante orange image collection, but it can reduce risk and improve the design of the physical flight tests.

## 5. Web Dashboards, Spatial Databases, and Asynchronous Processing Pipelines

The proposed system is not only a drone and computer vision model; it is an end-to-end orchard information system. Farmers need to register trees, select a target for scanning, view a map, review inspection history, and interpret fruit-count estimates. This requirement is consistent with broader precision-agriculture literature, where data collection is valuable only when converted into usable decision support. Soussi, Zero, Sacile, Trinchero, and Fossa (2024) reviewed smart sensors and smart data in precision agriculture and emphasized the convergence of IoT, big data analytics, artificial intelligence, and monitoring systems. UAV-based precision agriculture reviews likewise describe drones as sensing platforms whose value depends on image processing, data management, and actionable farm outputs (Tsouros, Bibi, & Sarigiannidis, 2019; Zhang & Kovacs, 2012).

Offboard computation is particularly relevant for a small drone prototype. A Raspberry Pi Zero 2 W and camera can capture images, synchronize timestamps, and transmit data, but deep neural network inference, 3D reconstruction, and dashboard processing may be more reliable on a ground laptop or cloud GPU. This design follows a common edge/offboard split: the drone performs lightweight sensing and flight-critical tasks, while heavier computer vision and record management run on nearby or cloud infrastructure. Raj, Singh, Astu, and Simmhan (2025) described a service-oriented drone-as-a-service framework in which UAV sensing, navigation, and analytics are abstracted as modular services across heterogeneous edge-cloud infrastructure. Although their work is not citrus-specific, it supports the architectural principle of decoupling aerial capture from scalable analytics.

For the proposed Perante orange system, the data pipeline can be expressed as:

```text
Drone/Raspberry Pi camera capture
-> Image and telemetry upload
-> Ground or cloud processing queue
-> YOLOv8/YOLOv11 detection
-> 2D tracking and 3D pose-guided duplicate reduction
-> Supabase/PostgreSQL database update
-> Real-time farmer dashboard update
```

This pipeline requires asynchronous job processing because drone image capture and AI inference do not always finish instantly. A farmer may request a scan, but the system must still wait for mission approval, drone takeoff, image capture, upload, inference, duplicate reduction, and result storage. Mission states such as `QUEUED`, `PROCESSING`, `COMPLETED`, `FAILED`, or `NEEDS_RESCAN` are therefore not only interface labels; they represent a practical state machine for managing long-running agricultural robotics tasks. A relational database can store farms, crops, trees, drones, scan missions, images, detections, fruit-count summaries, and inspection history. PostgreSQL with PostGIS or a Supabase-backed PostgreSQL deployment is appropriate because the system needs structured records, authentication, real-time subscriptions, and spatial/location fields for tree mapping. PostGIS extends PostgreSQL with geospatial storage, indexing, and querying capabilities (PostGIS Project, n.d.), while Supabase Realtime supports database-change subscriptions that can drive live dashboard updates (Supabase, n.d.).

The farmer dashboard also helps preserve accountability. A visible fruit count should be shown with scan date, tree ID, model confidence, image examples, and possible warnings such as low light, heavy occlusion, blocked route, or low confidence. This prevents the system from presenting a raw AI number as an unquestioned truth. The dashboard can also display orchard map markers using registered GPS or local coordinates, allowing the farmer to see tree-level inspection results across the farm. In this way, the web dashboard becomes the practical interface between drone autonomy, computer vision, database records, and farmer decision-making.

## 6. Synthesis and Research Gap

The reviewed literature shows that fruit detection using machine learning is already well established, with Faster R-CNN, YOLO, segmentation models, tracking algorithms, and structure-from-motion methods used for fruit detection, counting, and mapping (Bargoti & Underwood, 2017; Koirala et al., 2019; Liu et al., 2018; Sa et al., 2016). Research on citrus and orange counting also exists, including digital-image methods, deep-learning-based citrus tracking, and UAV-based citrus counting pipelines (Maldonado & Barbosa, 2016; Zhang et al., 2022; Zheng et al., 2023). Modern detector families such as YOLOv8 and YOLOv11 extend this direction by providing practical real-time architectures that can be trained on custom agricultural datasets (Jocher et al., 2023; Karacaoglu & Sahin, 2025; Khanam & Hussain, 2024). UAV orchard navigation has also been explored through vision-based row navigation, through-canopy flight, obstacle avoidance, target circumnavigation control, and foliage navigation using local sensors (Cao, 2015; Cao et al., 2013; Cui et al., 2016; Hulens et al., 2015; Stefas et al., 2019; Yang et al., 2025).

However, the literature does not fully address a localized Perante orange system for Nueva Vizcaya that combines every required component into one farmer-facing inspection pipeline. Many studies focus on one major component, such as detection accuracy, fruit counting, UAV navigation, simulation, or 3D mapping. Few studies connect all of these into a complete workflow where a farmer registers a specific tree, an autonomous drone approaches that registered tree, performs a canopy-centered circular scan, captures synchronized image and telemetry data, detects Perante oranges using locally annotated YOLO data, reduces duplicate counts through 2D tracking and 3D pose-guided projection, stores scan records in a relational spatial database, validates flight behavior in Gazebo SITL, and reports results through a real-time web dashboard.

The specific research gap is therefore the absence of an end-to-end Autonomous Drone-Based Citrus Fruit Counting and Orchard Mapping System for Perante Orange that integrates local dataset creation through Databox, farmer tree registration, ArduPilot/ArduCopter autonomous circular scanning, MAVLink-supported telemetry synchronization, 3D pose-guided duplicate-count reduction, empirical visible-count-to-yield modeling, Supabase/PostgreSQL database tracking, Gazebo Sim with ArduPilot SITL validation, and a real-time web dashboard for orchard decision support. The proposed project contributes by bringing these components together for a locally relevant Perante orange use case rather than treating detection, navigation, simulation, and reporting as isolated research tasks.

The project should still be scoped carefully. Its primary claim should be that it estimates visible fruit count from drone-captured images or video, with duplicate-count reduction and tree-level map records. It should avoid claiming that it perfectly counts every fruit on a tree because occlusion, lighting, wind, sensor noise, GPS degradation, and limited local training data may affect accuracy. A future extension may estimate total yield $\hat{Y}$ through regression models using visible count, canopy volume, and foliage density, but this requires field calibration and validation. In this way, the proposed research remains rigorous, locally grounded, and technically aligned with the real constraints of Perante orange orchard inspection.
