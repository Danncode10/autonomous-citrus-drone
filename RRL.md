# Review of Related Literature

## Background of the Study

Citrus production is a locally relevant agricultural concern for Nueva Vizcaya because the province is recognized as a major citrus-producing area in the Philippines. Idquival, Layog, Victoria, and Rosete (2023) identified Nueva Vizcaya as the country's main citrus producer and discussed how the citrus industry has been affected by land availability, temperature, precipitation, production cost, pests, disease, and climate-related pressures. Their analysis also noted that Kasibu, Nueva Vizcaya has land devoted to citrus cultivation and produces varieties that include Hamlin, Perante, and Valencia oranges. The Philippine Information Agency (2023) also reported that Vizcaya orange varieties are produced and sold from August to November, including Valencia and seedless Hamlin orange, also known locally as Perante Orange. DOST-PCAARRD (2018) further reported that citrus farming in Malabing Valley, Kasibu has contributed to employment and farmer livelihood in Nueva Vizcaya.

Because of this local importance, farmers need reliable ways to monitor the condition of their citrus trees. In a Perante orange orchard, the farmer may need to know how many fruits are visible on each tree, which trees are close to harvest, and which areas of the orchard may need attention. This information can help with harvest scheduling, labor planning, selling decisions, and farm record keeping. However, checking every tree manually can take a long time, especially when a farm has many trees. Manual checking may also vary from one person to another because fruit counting depends on the observer's position, eyesight, patience, and judgment of fruit maturity.

One major difficulty is that fruits are not arranged in a simple flat surface. Perante oranges grow around a tree canopy, meaning some fruits can be seen from the front, some from the left or right side, and some from the back. Some fruits may also be hidden behind leaves, branches, or other fruits. A person standing in one position may count only the fruits visible from that side, while missing fruits located deeper inside the canopy or on the opposite side. In the same way, a camera or drone that captures only one photo of a tree may not see the full fruit distribution. Therefore, one image is usually not enough to represent the whole tree.

A drone can help by taking images from different angles while moving around the tree. Instead of relying on one viewpoint, the drone may circle the tree and capture several photos or record a video. This can give the system more visual information because fruits hidden from one angle may become visible from another angle. For example, an orange hidden behind leaves in the front view may become visible when the drone moves to the side. This is why circular scanning is useful for the proposed project: it gives the computer vision model a better chance to observe the visible fruits around the canopy.

However, using many photos or a video also creates another problem. If the drone detects the same orange in one frame and then detects that same orange again in the next frame, the system may count it twice. For example, if one Perante orange appears in five consecutive video frames, a simple fruit detector may produce five detections even though there is only one real fruit. This problem is called repeated counting or duplicate counting across frames. It is important because a fruit detector by itself only identifies fruit-like objects inside an image; it does not automatically know whether a fruit in the current image is the same physical fruit seen in a previous image.

This means that the project cannot depend only on simple frame-by-frame detection. If every detected orange in every frame is added directly to the total count, the result may become too high. The system needs a way to connect detections across images. In computer vision, this can be done through object tracking, where the system follows the same detected fruit across nearby frames, or through multi-view association, where the system estimates whether detections from different angles belong to the same real fruit. Wang et al. (2019), for example, used MangoYOLO with a Kalman filter and the Hungarian algorithm to associate mango detections between neighboring video frames and reduce repeated counting. Liu et al. (2018) also combined deep learning, frame-to-frame tracking, and structure from motion to count fruits more reliably across image sequences.

Another difficulty is that the drone is moving while the tree, leaves, and fruits may also appear to move because of wind. Outdoor lighting can change as clouds pass or as sunlight creates shadows on the canopy. A fruit may be clear in one frame, blurry in another frame, and partly covered in the next frame. These field conditions make fruit detection more difficult than detecting objects in a controlled indoor image. Maldonado and Barbosa (2016) studied automatic green fruit counting in orange trees using digital images and showed that image-based counting can estimate visible fruit numbers, but the task remains affected by visibility and image conditions. These limitations show why the proposed system should include confidence values and should not claim perfect accuracy under all orchard conditions.

For these reasons, the present project should be framed as an autonomous drone-based system for estimating visible Perante orange count and visible harvest-readiness percentage. The word "visible" is important because some fruits may be fully hidden inside the canopy and cannot be detected by an RGB camera. The system can still be useful if it provides consistent tree-level estimates, stores inspection records, highlights trees that are likely ready for harvest, and helps farmers review orchard conditions more efficiently. The research problem therefore involves not only detecting fruits, but also deciding how the drone should capture the tree, how repeated detections should be handled, and how the final count should be reported in a way that is useful and honest for farm decision-making.

## Computer Vision and Machine Learning for Fruit Detection

Earlier computer-vision approaches for fruit detection used hand-crafted color, texture, and shape features, while more recent studies rely heavily on deep learning. Sa, Ge, Dayoub, Upcroft, Perez, and McCool (2016) developed DeepFruits, a fruit detection system using deep convolutional neural networks and Faster R-CNN for sweet pepper, rock melon, almond, apple, avocado, mango, and orange-like orchard targets. Their study showed that deep neural networks can provide accurate fruit detection in agricultural environments, making them useful for robotic harvesting, yield estimation, and autonomous agricultural platforms.

Bargoti and Underwood (2017) applied Faster R-CNN to fruit detection in orchards containing mangoes, almonds, and apples. They reported that data augmentation reduced the number of required training images by more than half and that tiling helped process orchard images containing hundreds of fruits. This is relevant to Perante orange detection because citrus-canopy images may contain many small fruit targets at different scales. Their results imply that the present project should include augmentation, multi-scale image handling, and local fine-tuning instead of relying only on a generic pretrained model.

Koirala, Walsh, Wang, and McCarthy (2019) benchmarked six deep learning architectures for mango detection and introduced MangoYOLO for real-time fruit detection and orchard fruit-load estimation. Their work is important because it demonstrated the suitability of YOLO-style detectors for real-time agricultural monitoring. Wang et al. (2019) extended this direction by combining MangoYOLO with Kalman filtering and the Hungarian algorithm to track mangoes across video frames, reducing repeated counting of the same fruit. For the present project, these findings suggest that a drone video workflow should not simply add frame-by-frame detections; it should use tracking or multi-view association to reduce double counting while the drone circles the tree.

Hani, Roy, and Isler (2020) compared fruit detection and counting methods for apple yield mapping and found that detection accuracy alone does not fully solve counting. Their work emphasized the importance of end-to-end counting methods that account for repeated observations across both sides of a tree row. Hani, Roy, and Isler (2020) also introduced MinneApple, a benchmark dataset for apple detection and segmentation, showing the value of shared annotated datasets for comparing fruit-detection methods. For a Perante orange project, this means the team should build a local annotated dataset because public apple, mango, or generic fruit datasets may not fully represent Perante orange color, size, canopy structure, camera angle, and lighting in Nueva Vizcaya.

Liu et al. (2019) proposed a monocular-camera fruit counting and mapping pipeline that used deep neural networks, semantic structure from motion, and semantic data association. Their study is relevant because it showed that a low-cost camera-based system can support fruit counting and mapping without always requiring expensive LiDAR and GPS/INS sensor suites. Liu et al. (2018) also combined deep segmentation, frame-to-frame tracking, and structure from motion to count fruit from image sequences, using Kalman-filter-assisted tracking and the Hungarian algorithm to limit duplicate counts. These studies support the idea that the project should connect fruit detection with camera pose, mapping, and tracking instead of treating each image as an isolated observation.

Recent citrus-specific studies further confirm that orange and citrus counting has already been researched. Zhang et al. (2022) proposed deep-learning-based in-field citrus fruit detection and tracking, using OrangeYOLO and OrangeSort to detect and track citrus fruits in orchard conditions. Zheng et al. (2023) proposed an online citrus counting system for large-scale unstructured orchards using UAVs and designed Citrus-YOLO for real-time citrus detection in video streams. These studies show that research on citrus fruit detection already exists internationally, including UAV-based citrus counting, but they also leave room for a localized Perante orange system that integrates farmer tree registration, autonomous circular scanning, harvest-readiness estimation, and a dashboard for tree-level records.

## Harvest Readiness and Ripeness Estimation

Fruit readiness estimation is related to but different from fruit detection. Detection answers where fruits are located, while readiness estimation attempts to classify maturity status using visible cues such as color, size, texture, and sometimes sensor-based measurements. Baglat, Hayat, Mendonca, Gupta, Mostafa, and Morgado-Dias (2023) reviewed non-destructive banana ripeness detection and found that both shallow machine learning and deep learning methods have been used for ripeness classification. Although banana ripeness differs from citrus maturity, their review supports the broader feasibility of non-destructive fruit maturity assessment using image-based machine learning.

For Perante orange, the proposed project should treat harvest readiness as an estimated visual class because outdoor lighting, shadows, camera exposure, and fruit occlusion may alter the appearance of citrus fruit. A practical model may begin with two classes, such as ready and not ready, and later expand into more detailed maturity levels if enough locally labeled images are available. The system should also report confidence values and flag uncertain results for human review, because readiness classification based only on RGB images can be less reliable than controlled laboratory methods or multispectral inspection.

## Autonomous UAV Navigation in Orchards

UAVs are useful for orchard monitoring because they can capture images from viewpoints that may be difficult for a ground worker or ground robot to reach. However, orchard flight is difficult because trees, branches, uneven terrain, wind, GPS error, and narrow spaces increase collision risk. Stefas, Bayram, and Isler (2019) studied vision-based monitoring of orchards with UAVs and developed a system for navigating inside orchard rows for crop inspection and yield-estimation tasks. Their work is directly related to the present project because it connects UAV navigation with orchard monitoring rather than using drones only for high-altitude mapping.

Hulens, Ribbens, Goedeme, and De Cubber (2015) studied real-time vision-based UAV navigation in fruit orchards using low-cost sensors such as a webcam. Their approach detected the center and end of orchard corridors so the UAV could move through rows without colliding with trees. This supports the idea that the project can begin with structured orchard navigation, waypoint guidance, and controlled scan behavior before attempting full autonomy in an unknown farm.

Yang, Ibrahimov, and Mueller (2025) presented an autonomous aerial system for through-the-canopy fruit counting using a high-fidelity simulation framework, a low-cost autonomy stack, and an RGB image-based fruit-counting workflow. Their system used simulation for global path planning, visual-inertial odometry for state estimation, depth-image-based local planning for obstacle avoidance, YOLOv8 for fruit detection, ByteTrack for tracking, and COLMAP for 3D reconstruction. Their findings are highly relevant because they show that orchard fruit counting requires the integration of navigation, obstacle avoidance, image collection, detection, tracking, and 3D reasoning.

For the specific behavior of circling a tree, UAV circumnavigation research provides useful control ideas. Cao, Muse, Casbeer, and Kingston (2013) proposed control algorithms that allow a UAV to circumnavigate an unknown target using range and range-rate measurements while maintaining a desired orbit radius. Cao (2015) further studied UAV circumnavigation in GPS-denied conditions using range-only measurement. These studies are not citrus-specific, but they provide a control-theory basis for the present project's tree-orbit behavior: the drone can maintain a desired distance from a target tree, move tangentially around it, and keep the tree inside the camera view.

Chen et al. (2023) developed a quadcopter drone for vision-based autonomous target following and obstacle avoidance. Their work supports the use of onboard camera information for target tracking, while obstacle avoidance remains active during flight. For a Perante orange inspection system, this means the circular scanning behavior should combine target-centered orbiting, distance keeping, image capture at multiple angles, and safety constraints around branches or nearby trees.

## Simulation Tools for Drone and Orchard Testing

Simulation is important for this project because real drone testing near trees can be risky, weather-dependent, and expensive. Gazebo is a widely used open-source robotics simulator that supports physics engines, realistic 3D rendering, sensor simulation, plugins, robot models, and command-line tools (Open Robotics, n.d.). Gazebo Classic documentation also states that it can generate sensor data from cameras, depth cameras, laser range finders, contact sensors, and other simulated sensors, which is useful for testing navigation, obstacle avoidance, and computer vision pipelines before field deployment.

PX4 documentation describes Gazebo as an open-source robotics simulator and identifies support for quadrotor, plane, VTOL, and rover simulation in software-in-the-loop workflows (PX4 Development Team, n.d.). Furrer, Burri, Achtelik, and Siegwart (2016) introduced RotorS as a modular Gazebo-based micro aerial vehicle simulator that supports multirotor models, controllers, state estimation, and simulated sensors. Xiao et al. (2020) also presented XTDrone, a customizable multi-rotor UAV simulation platform based on ROS, Gazebo, and PX4 for testing SLAM, object detection, motion planning, control, and multi-UAV cooperation. These studies support the choice of Gazebo or Gazebo with PX4/ROS as a simulation environment for the proposed citrus orchard drone system.

For the present project, Gazebo can be used to construct a simulated citrus orchard with virtual trees, terrain, obstacles, drone waypoints, camera sensors, and circular tree-scanning paths. The simulation can help test whether the drone can approach a registered tree, maintain a safe circular path, avoid obstacles, capture images from multiple angles, and return to a launch area. It can also support repeated experiments that would be difficult to perform safely in a real farm during early development.

## Synthesis and Research Gap

The reviewed literature shows that fruit detection using machine learning is already well established, with Faster R-CNN, YOLO, segmentation models, tracking algorithms, and structure-from-motion methods used for fruit detection, counting, and mapping (Bargoti & Underwood, 2017; Koirala et al., 2019; Liu et al., 2018; Sa et al., 2016). Research on citrus and orange counting also exists, including digital-image methods, deep-learning-based citrus tracking, and UAV-based citrus counting pipelines (Maldonado & Barbosa, 2016; Zhang et al., 2022; Zheng et al., 2023). UAV orchard navigation has also been explored through vision-based row navigation, through-canopy flight, obstacle avoidance, and target circumnavigation control (Cao, 2015; Cao et al., 2013; Hulens et al., 2015; Stefas et al., 2019; Yang et al., 2025).

However, the literature does not fully address a localized Perante orange system for Nueva Vizcaya that combines farmer-registered tree records, autonomous drone approach, circular tree scanning, visible fruit counting, harvest-readiness estimation, orchard map updating, and dashboard-based reporting. Many studies focus on one major component, such as detection accuracy, fruit counting, UAV navigation, or simulation. The proposed project therefore contributes by integrating these components into a practical tree-level monitoring workflow for Perante orange farms. The project should be scoped carefully by stating that it estimates visible fruit count and visible readiness from drone-captured images, while acknowledging that occlusion, lighting, wind, and limited local training data may affect the accuracy of total tree-level estimates.
